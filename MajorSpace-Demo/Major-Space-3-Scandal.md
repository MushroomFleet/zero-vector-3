# Major Space - The Exposé

## Hollywood Journalism at Its Most Cunning
**Location:** Various Los Angeles venues  
**Date:** June 27-29, 2025  
**Reporter:** Miranda Cross, The Industry Report  

### The Assignment

The chrome and glass offices of *The Industry Report* buzz with the relentless energy of modern entertainment journalism. Miranda Cross sits across from her editor, Ryan Walsh, in his corner office overlooking Sunset Boulevard. Trade magazines are scattered across his desk - *Variety*, *The Hollywood Reporter*, *Deadline* - all covering the same sanitized studio announcements and carefully managed celebrity interviews.

**RYAN:** Miranda, I need something with teeth. Real teeth. Not another puff piece about someone's "journey" or "transformation."

**MIRANDA:** *[leaning forward, interested]* What are you thinking?

**RYAN:** This Bob Martinez casting for "Major Space." Comedy actor gets the lead in a $200 million space western? There's got to be more to that story. Somebody got screwed, somebody made promises, somebody cut deals.

**MIRANDA:** *[already calculating]* Who else was up for it?

**RYAN:** Jack Reynolds - that method actor from the indie films. Steve Thompson - he's been trying to break out of action movies for years. Both had to be devastated. Wounded actors tell the best stories.

**MIRANDA:** *[slight smile]* Because they want validation that they deserved better.

**RYAN:** Exactly. I want you to find out what really happened in that casting room. Was it rigged? Did Martinez have connections? Did Shelley Morrison play favorites? There's always dirt if you know how to dig.

**MIRANDA:** Consider it done. Rejected actors are my specialty.

## The Hunter's Strategy

Miranda Cross doesn't rely on luck or accident. At 32, she's built her reputation on a simple formula: find people at their most vulnerable moments and make them feel safe enough to reveal what they shouldn't.

Her Harvard journalism degree taught her the technical skills, but her real education came from studying people - their insecurities, their need for validation, their desperate desire to believe their failures weren't their fault. She's made a career of being the sympathetic ear that actors use to process their disappointments, never realizing their confessions will become tomorrow's headlines.

**Miranda's Profile:**
- **Age:** 32
- **Education:** Harvard School of Journalism
- **Current Position:** Senior Entertainment Correspondent, The Industry Report
- **Specialty:** Investigative pieces on Hollywood power dynamics
- **Method:** Emotional manipulation disguised as empathetic journalism
- **Reputation:** The journalist you call when you want someone's career destroyed elegantly

**Her Strategy for Major Space:**
1. **Target Selection:** Focus on Jack and Steve - both have legitimate grievances to exploit
2. **Approach:** Never mention dirt-digging directly; frame interviews as industry think-pieces  
3. **Timing:** Strike while emotions are raw but not too fresh to seem opportunistic
4. **Location:** Choose settings where subjects feel comfortable and unguarded
5. **Manipulation:** Validate their disappointment while subtly suggesting systemic unfairness

## Interview #1: Jack Reynolds
**Date:** June 27, 2025  
**Time:** 2:30 PM  
**Location:** Grind Coffee, Brooklyn Heights  
**Angle:** "Art vs. Commerce in Modern Hollywood Casting"

The afternoon sun filters through the large windows of Grind Coffee, casting warm light across the exposed brick walls and reclaimed wood tables. Jack Reynolds sits in the corner booth, nursing his third espresso of the day. He's early - a habit from his theater days when being on time meant being late.

Miranda enters precisely on time, carrying a worn leather messenger bag and projecting the kind of understated confidence that suggests she belongs in any room she enters. She spots Jack immediately - the intensity radiating from him is unmistakable even in repose.

**MIRANDA:** Jack? Miranda Cross from The Industry Report. Thank you so much for agreeing to meet with me.

**JACK:** *[standing to shake hands]* Of course. Though I have to admit, I'm curious about this "art versus commerce" piece you mentioned.

**MIRANDA:** *[settling into the booth, pulling out a small digital recorder]* It's something I've been thinking about for months. The industry is at this fascinating crossroads where artistic merit and commercial viability don't always align. Do you mind if I record? It helps me capture the nuance of what you're saying.

**JACK:** Sure, go ahead.

**MIRANDA:** *[starting the recorder]* So let's start broad. You've built an incredible reputation in independent film for your commitment to character depth and psychological authenticity. When you approach a mainstream role like Major Harrison, how do you balance artistic integrity with commercial expectations?

**JACK:** *[relaxing, this is his element]* That's the eternal question, isn't it? I believe authentic character work serves both purposes. If you find the truth in a character, audiences respond to that truth whether it's in a $50,000 indie film or a $200 million blockbuster.

**MIRANDA:** That's beautifully put. And that preparation you do - I've heard you develop complete backstories, work with consultants, really immerse yourself in the character's world?

**JACK:** *[lighting up]* For Harrison, I spent months researching frontier psychology, military trauma, survivor's guilt. I interviewed veterans, studied isolation's effects on the human psyche. By the time I walked into that audition, I knew exactly who Harrison was - not just what was in the script, but who he was as a complete human being.

**MIRANDA:** *[leaning forward, genuinely impressed]* That level of preparation is extraordinary. It must have been incredibly frustrating when... well, when the role went in a different direction.

**JACK:** *[pause, choosing words carefully]* Disappointment is part of this business. I've learned to separate my attachment to a role from the reality of casting decisions.

**MIRANDA:** *[sympathetic tone]* But this one felt different, didn't it? I mean, Harrison seems tailor-made for the kind of psychological depth you bring to characters.

**JACK:** *[defensive walls lowering slightly]* It did feel like a perfect match, yes. The character's journey from guilt-ridden isolation to reluctant leadership - that's exactly the kind of complex arc I love exploring.

**MIRANDA:** And now Bob Martinez gets to explore it instead. *[pause, letting that hang]* I'm curious about your thoughts on that choice. Martinez is incredibly talented, obviously, but it's quite a departure from comedy to something this psychologically demanding.

**JACK:** *[careful]* Bob's a skilled actor. Comedy requires tremendous timing and emotional truth. Those skills are transferable.

**MIRANDA:** *[nodding thoughtfully]* Absolutely. Though I imagine there's a part of you that wonders about the decision-making process. When someone with your specific preparation and background for this exact type of role doesn't get it... it makes you question whether the choice was based purely on artistic merit.

**JACK:** *[pause, then more honest than he intended]* I'd be lying if I said I didn't wonder about that. When you've done the work, when you understand the character that deeply, and the role goes to someone whose strength lies in a completely different area... you do question the criteria being used.

**MIRANDA:** *[very gently]* Are you suggesting the decision might have been more about marketability than artistic fit?

**JACK:** *[realizing he's ventured into dangerous territory]* I'm not suggesting anything. Every casting director has their reasons. Shelley Morrison is respected in this industry.

**MIRANDA:** *[backing off, but filing away his slip]* Of course, of course. And that's what makes it fascinating from a journalistic perspective - these decisions shape not just individual careers but the entire landscape of what stories get told and how.

**JACK:** *[relieved to move to safer ground]* Exactly. The choices casting directors make today determine what audiences see five years from now.

**MIRANDA:** One last question - and this is more philosophical. Do you think there's pressure on casting directors to prioritize commercial appeal over the artistic fit between actor and character?

**JACK:** *[thoughtful pause]* I think... I think everyone in this industry faces pressure to balance art and commerce. Whether you're a director, a producer, or a casting director, you're always weighing artistic vision against market realities. Sometimes those align perfectly, sometimes they don't.

**MIRANDA:** And when they don't align, which way do the scales typically tip?

**JACK:** *[small, rueful smile]* I think we both know the answer to that.

**MIRANDA:** *[turning off recorder]* Jack, this has been incredibly insightful. You've given me a lot to think about regarding the artistic process versus industry pressures.

**JACK:** I hope it's useful for your piece. And Miranda? I hope I didn't say anything that came across as bitter. That wasn't my intention.

**MIRANDA:** Not at all. You came across as thoughtful and professional - exactly how a serious actor should discuss these complex issues.

*[They shake hands and Jack leaves, feeling like he's contributed to an important conversation about artistic integrity in Hollywood. He has no idea he's just handed Miranda the foundation for a story about casting corruption.]*

## Interview #2: Steve Thompson  
**Date:** June 29, 2025  
**Time:** 7:00 PM  
**Location:** Nobu Malibu  
**Angle:** "Breaking Free from Genre Typecasting"

Nobu Malibu at sunset is Hollywood power dining at its most refined. The restaurant overlooks the Pacific, where the evening light turns the waves into liquid gold. Miranda chose this location carefully - upscale enough to make Steve feel respected, intimate enough for honest conversation.

Steve Thompson sits at a corner table, already working on his first sake. He's dressed casually but expensively - the kind of effortless style that comes from having a personal shopper and unlimited resources. Miranda approaches, noting the slight tension in his shoulders that suggests this interview matters to him.

**MIRANDA:** Steve! Thank you for making time for this. I know you're incredibly busy.

**STEVE:** *[standing to greet her]* Miranda, right? Thanks for choosing somewhere with a view. After the day I've had, I needed this.

**MIRANDA:** *[settling into her seat]* Bad day?

**STEVE:** *[rueful laugh]* Just the usual Hollywood shuffle. Meetings about meetings about projects that may never happen. You know how it is.

**MIRANDA:** *[signaling for the recorder]* Actually, that's exactly what I want to talk about tonight. This piece I'm working on is about actors breaking free from genre expectations - how difficult it is to be seen beyond the box the industry puts you in. Do you mind if I record?

**STEVE:** Go for it. And yeah, that's definitely something I can speak to.

**MIRANDA:** *[starting recording]* You've built an incredible career in action films. Box office success, international recognition, franchise potential. But I sense there's more you want to explore as an actor.

**STEVE:** *[immediately engaged]* Absolutely. Action films have been great to me - I'm not complaining about the success. But I got into acting to explore the full range of human experience, not just the guy who saves the world through superior firepower.

**MIRANDA:** What drew you specifically to dramatic work? Was there a moment when you realized you wanted to expand beyond action?

**STEVE:** *[taking a sip of sake]* I think it was during "Midnight Crossing" - I had these three dramatic scenes that required real vulnerability. For the first time in years, I felt like I was actually acting, not just hitting marks and delivering one-liners. I remembered why I fell in love with this craft in the first place.

**MIRANDA:** And that led you to pursue more dramatic opportunities?

**STEVE:** *[nodding]* I've been studying with Stella Martinez for two years. Shakespeare, method acting, scene study - really working on my craft beyond just the physical demands of action work.

**MIRANDA:** That's serious commitment. When an opportunity like Major Harrison comes along - a role that blends action with real psychological depth - it must feel like exactly what you've been preparing for.

**STEVE:** *[his energy shifting slightly]* It really did. Harrison isn't just an action hero; he's a broken man trying to rebuild himself. The space western setting allows for physical storytelling but demands emotional complexity. It seemed perfect for what I've been working toward.

**MIRANDA:** *[carefully]* And then Bob Martinez gets the role. How do you process that as an actor who's been specifically preparing for this kind of transition?

**STEVE:** *[pause, then honest]* Look, Bob's talented. I respect him. But it's hard not to wonder if the industry can see past the action star label when they're making these decisions.

**MIRANDA:** What do you mean by that?

**STEVE:** *[warming to the topic]* I have two years of intensive dramatic training with one of the best coaches in the city. I've studied the character, worked with veterans to understand the military background, developed a completely different physicality for Harrison. But at the end of the day, I'm still "the action guy" and Bob is seen as someone making an artistic transition.

**MIRANDA:** *[sympathetic]* That must be incredibly frustrating - to have done all that preparation and still feel like you're not being seen for who you are as an actor.

**STEVE:** *[more animated now]* Exactly! It's like there's this assumption that action stars can't handle serious dramatic material, while comedy actors are automatically given credit for depth and range.

**MIRANDA:** Do you think that reflects a bias in the industry?

**STEVE:** *[pause, then more carefully]* I think there are definitely preconceptions. Comedy is seen as more intellectually respectable than action, even though both require specific skills and training.

**MIRANDA:** *[pressing gently]* When you say preconceptions, are you talking about casting directors specifically?

**STEVE:** *[hesitating]* I'm talking about the industry in general. Look, I don't want to criticize anyone personally, but when someone like me can audition with extensive preparation and dramatic training, and still lose out to someone transitioning from comedy... it makes you wonder what criteria are really being used.

**MIRANDA:** You're being very diplomatic, but I can hear the frustration underneath. After everything you've invested in expanding your range, to have that not seem to matter...

**STEVE:** *[more emotional than intended]* You want to know the truth? I bare my soul in that audition room. I show them vulnerability, preparation, genuine understanding of the character. And the feedback is essentially "great work, but we're going with the comedy guy." It makes you question whether dramatic ability actually matters, or if it's all about perception and marketability.

**MIRANDA:** When you put it that way, it does sound like there might be some systemic issues with how actors are categorized and evaluated.

**STEVE:** *[realizing he may have said too much]* I don't want this to sound like sour grapes. Every casting director has their reasons, and I respect Shelley Morrison's experience.

**MIRANDA:** *[smoothly moving past his attempt to backtrack]* Of course. But your point about systemic issues is fascinating. Do you think there's pressure on casting directors to make "safe" choices based on public perception rather than actual ability?

**STEVE:** *[thoughtful]* I think everyone in this town faces pressure to minimize risk. A comedy actor doing drama feels like a "brave artistic choice." An action star doing drama feels like a "risky experiment." Even when the actual preparation and ability might suggest otherwise.

**MIRANDA:** That's a really astute observation about how the industry frames these transitions differently based on the actor's background.

**STEVE:** *[slightly bitter]* Yeah, well, I've had time to think about it.

**MIRANDA:** *[turning off recorder]* Steve, this has been incredibly enlightening. Your perspective on genre bias and industry perceptions - it's exactly the kind of insight that makes for compelling journalism.

**STEVE:** I hope I didn't come across as bitter. That's not who I am.

**MIRANDA:** Not at all. You came across as thoughtful and analytical - someone who understands the business side of the industry while still being passionate about the craft.

*[They finish dinner with small talk about upcoming projects. Steve leaves feeling like he's contributed to an important conversation about typecasting in Hollywood. He has no idea he's just provided Miranda with ammunition for a story about bias and unfair casting practices.]*

## The Article Takes Shape

Back in her West Hollywood apartment, Miranda Cross spreads her notes across her glass dining table. The city twinkles beyond her floor-to-ceiling windows as she begins crafting the story that could define her career.

**Working Headline:** "The Major Space Controversy: When Hollywood Casting Prioritizes Perception Over Preparation"

**Article Angle:**
Two extensively prepared actors - one a method actor with perfect psychological insight, one an action star with years of dramatic training - passed over for a comedy actor in what sources suggest was a decision based more on marketing appeal than artistic merit.

**Key Elements:**
- Jack's months of character research versus Bob's comedy background
- Steve's two years of dramatic training at the Actor's Workshop
- Both actors questioning the "criteria being used" in casting decisions
- Industry bias against certain types of actors seeking career transitions
- The pressure on casting directors to make "safe" commercial choices

**Miranda's Strategy:**
- Never directly quote anything that sounds like personal attacks
- Focus on systemic issues and industry bias rather than individual blame
- Use their own words to suggest unfairness without explicitly stating it
- Frame it as investigative journalism exposing broader problems

**The Hook:**
"When a $200 million space western chooses marketability over method, what does it say about the state of artistic integrity in modern Hollywood?"

Miranda smiles as she begins typing. By tomorrow morning, Shelley Morrison's casting decision will be under a microscope, and two disappointed actors will discover their private frustrations have become public ammunition in Hollywood's ongoing war between art and commerce.

They'll never see it coming.
