# Major Space - Casting Sessions

## Movie Premise

**Title:** Major Space  
**Genre:** Space Western  
**Director:** TBD  
**Producers:** Apex Studios  

### Synopsis

Set in the year 2387, "Major Space" follows the story of a lone gunslinger navigating the lawless frontier planets of a distant galaxy. When a mysterious alien artifact threatens to destroy entire star systems, our protagonist must overcome their past failures and unite scattered settlements to prevent galactic catastrophe. 

The film blends classic western themes of justice, redemption, and frontier survival with the wonder and isolation of space exploration. Think "The Good, The Bad and The Ugly" meets "Blade Runner 2049."

### Lead Character: Major Jake "Dusty" Harrison

**Background:** A former galactic peace officer turned bounty hunter after a tragic mission failure cost the lives of his team. He wanders the outer rim planets, taking jobs that pay well and keep him moving. Haunted by his past but maintaining a strict moral code.

**Character Requirements:**
- Physical presence and authenticity in action sequences
- Emotional depth to portray guilt, redemption, and growth
- Ability to convey isolation and inner conflict
- Chemistry with ensemble cast of settlers and outlaws
- Comfort with both futuristic and western elements

**Key Scenes:**
- Philosophical moments of solitude under alien stars
- High-stakes gunfights with advanced weaponry
- Emotional confrontations about past failures
- Leadership moments rallying diverse groups
- Final sacrifice/redemption arc

## Interview Sessions

### Session 1: Jack Reynolds Interview
**Date:** June 23, 2025  
**Duration:** 45 minutes  
**Interviewer:** Shelley Morrison  

**SHELLEY:** Jack, please come in and have a seat. I've read your materials and I'm impressed by your independent film work. Tell me, what draws you to this space western role, and how do you see Major Jake 'Dusty' Harrison as a character? I'm particularly interested in your interpretation of his psychological journey from guilt-ridden loner to reluctant leader.

**JACK:** Thank you, Shelley. *[settles into chair, focused intensity]* What draws me to this role is the profound psychological complexity underneath what could easily be just another action hero. Major Harrison represents something universal - the weight of leadership failure and the possibility of redemption. 

I've spent weeks researching frontier psychology and the effects of isolation on the human psyche. Harrison isn't just wandering the galaxy; he's running from himself. The space setting amplifies the western theme of the lone gunslinger, but adds this existential dimension - you're literally floating in the void, surrounded by the infinite. That isolation would either break a man or forge him into something stronger.

**SHELLEY:** That's a fascinating interpretation. How do you see his relationship with guilt specifically?

**JACK:** Guilt is his constant companion, more faithful than any partner. I imagine Harrison carries the weight of every decision that led to his team's death. He probably replays those moments endlessly - was there something different he could have done? A different order he could have given? 

But here's what I find compelling - guilt can be a prison or a teacher. For Harrison, I believe it's both. It keeps him isolated, but it also drives his moral code. He won't let others pay for his mistakes again. Every job he takes, every person he helps or refuses to help, is filtered through that lens of "never again."

**SHELLEY:** You mentioned you've done extensive research. Walk me through your preparation process.

**JACK:** *[leans forward, passionate]* I've immersed myself in the psychology of military veterans dealing with survivor's guilt. I've studied the mythology of the American West and how frontier narratives translate to space. I watched everything from "The Searchers" to "Solaris" to understand how isolation functions differently in westerns versus science fiction.

I've also developed a complete backstory for Harrison - not just what's in the script, but his childhood, his military service, the exact details of the mission that went wrong. I know how he drinks his coffee, which hand he draws with, what nightmares wake him up. Method acting isn't just about the performance; it's about becoming the person who would give that performance.

**SHELLEY:** That level of preparation is exactly what this role demands. Now, this is a big-budget studio film - very different from your independent work. How do you feel about that transition?

**JACK:** *[pauses thoughtfully]* I won't lie - it's intimidating. Independent films give you the luxury of multiple takes and intimate collaboration. Studio films are about precision under pressure. But I think that pressure actually serves Harrison's character. He's a man who performs under impossible circumstances. If I can channel that energy, that need to get it right when everything's on the line, it'll inform the performance.

I also understand the commercial expectations. This isn't just about artistic expression; it's about creating a character audiences will connect with across multiple demographics. But I believe authentic character work serves both purposes. If I find the truth in Harrison, audiences will feel it.

**SHELLEY:** Let's talk about the action sequences. You're known for dramatic work, but this role requires significant physical presence.

**JACK:** I've been training extensively - not just physically, but studying how movement reflects character. Harrison moves like a man carrying invisible weight. His gun draw isn't just fast; it's reluctant. Every action sequence tells part of his story.

I've worked with military consultants to understand how someone with Harrison's background would approach combat. He's not reckless - his team died because of a tactical error, so now he overcompensates with preparation. Every fight should feel like a chess match he's already played out in his head.

**SHELLEY:** Excellent. One final question - this is potentially a franchise-launching role. How do you feel about that kind of commitment?

**JACK:** *[strong, determined]* I'm ready for it. I believe in this character and this story. If audiences respond, I'd be honored to continue exploring Harrison's journey. There's so much depth to mine - his relationships, his past, his growth as a leader. I see this as a career-defining opportunity, and I don't take that lightly.

**SHELLEY:** Thank you, Jack. That was excellent. We'll be in touch soon.

**JACK:** Thank you for your time, Shelley. I hope to hear from you.

*[End of Interview - Duration: 45 minutes]*

**Shelley's Notes:**
- Exceptional preparation and character analysis
- Deep understanding of genre requirements  
- Thoughtful approach to action sequences
- Strong commitment to franchise potential
- Needs to prove himself in big-budget environment
- Most psychologically sophisticated interpretation so far

### Session 2: Bob Martinez Interview
**Date:** June 23, 2025  
**Duration:** 40 minutes  
**Interviewer:** Shelley Morrison  

**SHELLEY:** Bob, great to meet you. I have to say, I'm a fan of your work on "City Nights" - you had incredible chemistry with that ensemble cast. But today we're talking about something completely different. What made you want to transition into dramatic work, specifically this space western?

**BOB:** *[warm smile, then more serious tone]* Thank you, Shelley. "City Nights" was a dream job, but after fifteen years in comedy, I felt like I was only showing audiences one side of who I am as an actor. Comedy taught me timing, how to find truth in absurd situations, and how to connect with people instantly. But I've always believed those skills translate to drama - they're just applied differently.

As for "Major Space," the genre blend fascinated me. Westerns are about people stripped down to their essential selves, facing impossible odds. Comedy is about finding humanity in flawed characters. Harrison is both - a deeply flawed man trying to do right in an impossible situation. That resonates with me.

**SHELLEY:** That's an interesting perspective. How do you see your comedy background informing this dramatic role?

**BOB:** Harrison can't be humorless - no one survives trauma without some way to deflect or cope. I don't mean he's cracking jokes during gunfights, but there's a bitter irony to his situation. A peace officer who couldn't keep peace. A leader whose leadership failed. A man seeking redemption in the most unredeemable places in the galaxy.

My improv training taught me to listen and react truthfully in the moment. In comedy, if you don't believe your own ridiculous situation, the audience won't either. Same principle applies here - if I don't believe in Harrison's pain, his guilt, his gradual return to hope, neither will they.

**SHELLEY:** You mention his gradual return to hope. How do you see that character arc playing out?

**BOB:** *[leans forward, engaged]* It's not a straight line, right? Harrison probably has moments where he connects with people - maybe helping settlers defend their colony, or seeing potential in a young recruit. But then guilt pulls him back. "I don't deserve this. People around me get hurt."

The beautiful thing about the space setting is that isolation becomes literal. He's not just emotionally distant; he's physically separated by vast distances between worlds. When he finally chooses to stay, to fight, to lead again - that's not just an emotional choice, it's a physical one. He's literally choosing to remain tethered to people instead of drifting in the void.

**SHELLEY:** Let's talk about the action elements. This isn't exactly "City Nights."

**BOB:** *[grins]* No, definitely not. But I've been training - stunt work, weapons handling, physical conditioning. What excites me is that the action serves the character. Harrison's not showing off; every fight is reluctant necessity. There's weight to it.

I think my comedy background actually helps here. Physical comedy requires precise timing and spatial awareness. A gunfight is choreography - it's about rhythm, reaction, and making it look effortless while being completely controlled. Plus, I'm used to ensemble work. Action sequences are team efforts.

**SHELLEY:** This would be a significant departure for your career. How do you think audiences will respond to seeing you in this type of role?

**BOB:** *[thoughtful pause]* There's definitely risk. Some people will come in expecting the guy from "City Nights." But I think there's also opportunity - audiences love seeing actors surprise them. Robin Williams in "Good Will Hunting," Jim Carrey in "Eternal Sunshine." Comedy actors often bring a vulnerability to dramatic roles because we're comfortable being human and flawed on screen.

I've been studying Harrison for months. I've watched every space western, every redemption story. I understand this character's journey isn't just about proving I can do drama - it's about serving the story and honoring what Harrison represents to audiences who need to believe in second chances.

**SHELLEY:** Speaking of studying the character - walk me through your preparation process.

**BOB:** I started with the emotional core - what does guilt feel like when you're responsible for people's deaths? I talked to veterans, read memoirs, studied PTSD research. Then I worked outward - how does that internal state affect how he moves, speaks, relates to others?

I've also been working with a dialect coach on the subtle ways speech patterns change when someone's been alone for extended periods. And I've been studying astronomy, actually. If Harrison's wandering between star systems, he'd know the night sky like a roadmap. Those details matter.

**SHELLEY:** That's impressive preparation. Now, this is potentially a franchise starter. How do you feel about that kind of long-term commitment?

**BOB:** *[excited but measured]* It's exactly what I want. Comedy taught me about creating characters people want to spend time with, week after week, season after season. With Harrison, there's so much to explore - his past, his relationships, how he builds this new community of allies. 

A franchise means really getting to dig deep, to show different facets of the character as situations change. That's an actor's dream - having the time to really inhabit someone and grow with them.

**SHELLEY:** Excellent. Any final thoughts on why you should be our Major Harrison?

**BOB:** *[direct, confident]* Because I understand that heroism isn't about being fearless - it's about doing the right thing despite being afraid. Harrison's not a traditional action hero; he's a human being who's been broken and is choosing to heal by helping others. That's a story worth telling, and I'm ready to tell it with everything I've got.

**SHELLEY:** Thank you, Bob. That was terrific. We'll be in touch.

**BOB:** Thank you, Shelley. I really appreciate the opportunity.

*[End of Interview - Duration: 40 minutes]*

**Shelley's Notes:**
- Surprisingly thoughtful dramatic interpretation
- Strong understanding of character arc and genre requirements
- Comedy background brings unique vulnerability and timing
- Excellent preparation and research approach
- Natural charisma could translate well to leading man appeal
- Franchise commitment is genuine and well-considered
- Potential risk: audience preconceptions from comedy work

### Session 3: Steve Thompson Interview
**Date:** June 23, 2025  
**Duration:** 50 minutes  
**Interviewer:** Shelley Morrison  

**SHELLEY:** Steve, thank you for coming in. I'll be honest - when your name came up for this role, there were some raised eyebrows. Not because of your talent, but because this is quite a departure from your recent work. What draws you to this project?

**STEVE:** *[confident but humble]* Shelley, I appreciate the honesty, and I understand the skepticism. For the past decade, I've built a career on action films, and I'm proud of that work. But I didn't get into acting to be limited to one type of role. "Major Space" represents everything I've been working toward - a character with real emotional depth set in a world that still allows for the physical storytelling I love.

Harrison isn't just an action hero; he's a broken man trying to rebuild himself. That's the challenge I want as an actor - to show audiences there's more to me than explosions and one-liners.

**SHELLEY:** Let's address the elephant in the room. There's a perception that action stars can't handle serious dramatic material. How do you respond to that?

**STEVE:** *[leans forward, passionate]* I think that's a lazy assumption. Look at my film "Midnight Crossing" - I had three dramatic scenes that required real vulnerability. Or "Ironclad," where my character dealt with PTSD. Action films today aren't just mindless spectacle; they require actors who can ground fantastical situations in human emotion.

But I'll admit, I want to prove myself further. That's why I've been studying with Stella Martinez at the Actor's Workshop for two years. I've done Shakespeare readings, method acting exercises, scene study. I'm not just an action star trying to wing it - I'm an actor who's ready to show his full range.

**SHELLEY:** Tell me about your preparation for this specific role.

**STEVE:** Harrison's military background resonates with me personally - my father was a Marine. I've spent time with combat veterans dealing with command responsibility and survivor's guilt. I understand the weight of having people's lives in your hands and what it means when you fail them.

Physically, I've been working with a movement coach to develop a different physicality for Harrison. He's not the confident action hero I usually play. He moves like someone carrying invisible wounds - hesitant, careful, always ready to run. Every gesture should tell his story.

I've also been studying westerns and space films religiously. "Unforgiven" for the guilt and redemption themes, "Blade Runner" for existential isolation, "The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance" for the cost of heroism. This genre blend requires understanding both traditions.

**SHELLEY:** That's impressive preparation. Walk me through how you see Harrison's emotional journey.

**STEVE:** *[thoughtful, measured]* Harrison starts the film as a ghost. He's going through the motions of living but he's not really alive. Every decision is filtered through "How do I avoid responsibility? How do I stay detached?" 

The turning point comes when he's forced to care again - maybe it's a young settler who reminds him of a lost team member, or a community that trusts him despite his warnings. He realizes isolation isn't protection; it's cowardice.

The physical confrontations in the film mirror this emotional journey. Early fights are desperate, sloppy, just survival. By the end, when he's fighting for something beyond himself, there's precision and purpose. The action serves the character growth.

**SHELLEY:** Let's talk about the space western elements specifically. How do you approach that genre blend?

**STEVE:** The western elements ground the character in familiar archetypes - the lone gunslinger, the reluctant sheriff, the man with a dark past. Audiences understand that language immediately. The space setting adds scope and wonder, but also emphasizes the isolation. 

In a traditional western, you can ride to the next town. In space, the next settlement might be light-years away. That physical isolation amplifies the emotional themes. Harrison isn't just avoiding people; he's avoiding entire worlds.

The technology serves the western themes too. Advanced weapons make gunfights more lethal, faster. Communication across space makes leadership decisions more critical. It's familiar but elevated.

**SHELLEY:** You mentioned wanting to show more range. What specifically can you bring to this role that we might not expect?

**STEVE:** *[vulnerable, honest]* Stillness. In action films, I'm constantly moving, fighting, pursuing. Harrison requires the ability to convey volumes with subtle changes in expression. Some of his most important moments will be quiet - staring at alien stars, remembering his team, choosing to stay when every instinct says run.

I can also bring authenticity to the leadership aspects. I've been the lead on major productions; I understand the weight of having hundreds of people depending on your decisions. That pressure, that responsibility - it's not that different from what Harrison faces.

**SHELLEY:** This could be a career-defining role for you. How important is this transition to more dramatic work?

**STEVE:** *[direct, passionate]* This is everything to me. I love action films, but I don't want to be fifty years old still playing the same character. I want longevity, and that requires range. 

"Major Space" is perfect because it honors my action background while demanding more. I'm not running from what I've done; I'm building on it. Harrison is the role that could show audiences I'm an actor who happens to be in action films, not just an action star.

**SHELLEY:** What about the franchise potential? This could be a long-term commitment.

**STEVE:** I'm absolutely ready for that. A franchise allows for real character development over time. Look at what Robert Downey Jr. did with Tony Stark - each film revealed new layers. Harrison has that potential. His past, his relationships, his growth as a leader - there are years of stories to explore.

Plus, I understand the business side. My action films have proven I can open a movie internationally. That commercial appeal, combined with stronger dramatic material, could create something special.

**SHELLEY:** Final question - why should Harrison be Steve Thompson?

**STEVE:** *[confident but earnest]* Because I understand him. I've carried responsibility for others and felt the weight when things went wrong. I know what it's like to be seen as one thing when you're capable of more. Harrison's journey from isolation to leadership, from guilt to redemption - that's not just a character arc for me, it's personal growth.

I have the physical presence for the action, the work ethic for the preparation, and the hunger to prove I can handle the dramatic demands. Most importantly, I believe in this character and this story. Harrison deserves an actor who sees him as more than just a role - he's a chance to show that everyone deserves a second chance to be their best self.

**SHELLEY:** Thank you, Steve. That was excellent. We'll be in touch.

**STEVE:** Thank you, Shelley. I really appreciate the opportunity to show you who I am as an actor.

*[End of Interview - Duration: 50 minutes]*

**Shelley's Notes:**
- Surprisingly vulnerable and self-aware about career perceptions
- Extensive dramatic training and preparation beyond public knowledge
- Strong understanding of character's emotional journey
- Physical presence and leadership experience are genuine assets
- Commercial appeal could benefit franchise launch

## Final Casting Decision

### Shelley Morrison's Private Evaluation
**Date:** June 23, 2025  
**Time:** 11:30 PM  
**Decision Due:** June 24, 2025 - 9:00 AM  

After conducting three exceptional interviews today, I find myself facing one of the most difficult casting decisions of my career. Each candidate brings unique strengths to the role of Major Jake "Dusty" Harrison, and any of them could potentially succeed. However, the stakes are enormous - this is a franchise-launching, $200 million production that needs to work both artistically and commercially.

**Individual Assessment:**

**Jack Reynolds** delivered the most psychologically sophisticated interpretation of Harrison's character. His method acting preparation was extraordinary - the depth of research into frontier psychology, military trauma, and survivor's guilt was impressive. He truly understands the emotional journey from guilt-ridden loner to reluctant leader. However, this would be his first major studio film, and that transition from independent films represents a significant risk. While his artistic approach is unquestionable, can he handle the pressure and scale of a blockbuster production?

**Bob Martinez** surprised me with his thoughtful dramatic interpretation. His comedy background actually brings a unique vulnerability and understanding of flawed characters that could serve Harrison well. His preparation was excellent - veteran interviews, dialect coaching, even astronomy research. He has natural charisma and franchise appeal. The risk lies in audience preconceptions about comedy actors in serious dramatic roles, though he's clearly aware of this challenge.

**Steve Thompson** was the biggest surprise of the day. Beneath the action star exterior is an actor with genuine dramatic training and deep preparation. His personal connection to military background through his father brings authenticity. He has the physical presence and proven commercial appeal that could launch a franchise globally. Most importantly, he demonstrated the "stillness" and vulnerability that Harrison requires. The question is whether audiences can see past his action star image.

**The Decision:**

After much consideration, I am recommending **Bob Martinez** for the role of Major Jake "Dusty" Harrison.

**Rationale:**

1. **Artistic Merit**: Bob's understanding of Harrison as a deeply flawed human being seeking redemption resonates perfectly with the character's journey. His comedy background brings an unexpected vulnerability that could make Harrison more accessible to audiences.

2. **Genre Fit**: His insight about space westerns blending isolation with human connection shows he truly understands what makes this story special. His interpretation of Harrison's gradual return to hope feels authentic and earned.

3. **Risk vs. Reward**: While there's risk in casting a comedy actor in a dramatic role, the potential reward is enormous. Audiences love seeing actors surprise them, and Bob has the preparation and commitment to deliver.

4. **Franchise Potential**: His experience creating characters that audiences want to spend time with across multiple seasons translates perfectly to franchise filmmaking. He understands the long-term commitment required.

5. **Commercial Viability**: Bob has proven appeal with general audiences while bringing dramatic depth that could earn critical respect. This combination is exactly what "Major Space" needs.

6. **Chemistry Factor**: Of the three candidates, Bob has the most natural warmth and charisma, which will be crucial for Harrison's relationships with the ensemble cast.

**Runner-up**: Steve Thompson came very close. His dramatic training and commercial appeal make him an excellent alternative if Bob declines or if the studio insists on a bigger name for international markets.

**The Call**: I'll phone Bob's agent first thing tomorrow morning.
